## B. Grammar curriculum

The guide explicitly presents five grammar lessons, in this order, on pages 2–3. Each remains a separate, focused lesson.

| Lesson | Title and topics | Rules and important exceptions | Examples present in PDF | Source pages |
|---:|---|---|---|---:|
| 1 | **Pronouns — Who and Whom.** Subject versus object; object of a preposition; he/him substitution test; interrupting phrases. | **Who** is the subject/doer (he/she/they). **Whom** receives an action or follows a preposition (him/her/them). Substitute *he* or *him*: *he* → who; *him* → whom. Ignore interrupters such as “do you think” and “did you say.” | “Who won the debate? → He won.” “To whom did you give the award? → to him.” “Who do you think will be chosen? → He will be chosen → who.” | 2 |
| 2 | **Consistent Verb Voice and Mood.** Indicative, imperative, conditional/subjunctive mood; consistency of mood and voice within a sentence. | Keep the same voice (active/passive) and mood within one sentence. A command joined with *and* should be followed by another command. **If … were** pairs with **would + verb**. | Mood table: “The bus leaves at seven”; “Finish your essay and submit it”; “If it were my decision, I would wait.” Correct/incorrect pairs include “Finish your essay, and submit it” and “If it were my decision, I would postpone the trip.” | 2 |
| 3 | **Verbals — Infinitives and Infinitive Phrases.** Form and noun, adjective, or adverb functions. | An infinitive is **to + base verb**. An infinitive phrase includes words completing it. A **to + noun** phrase is a prepositional phrase, not an infinitive. | “To travel to Mars is his dream” (noun); “Omar had a speech to deliver” (adjective); “They arrived early to set up the tables” (adverb); contrasts with “to the library” and “to his teacher.” | 3 |
| 4 | **Verbs — Active and Passive Voice.** Identify who performs or receives an action; convert passive to active. | Active: subject does the action. Passive: subject receives it. Passive pattern: a form of **be** (including *is, was, were, has been*) + past participle + optional *by* phrase. Conversion: find the doer after *by*, move it to the front, use a simple verb. | “The mayor opened the new library” / “The new library was opened by the mayor.” “The trophy was lifted by the captain” → “The captain lifted the trophy.” | 3 |
| 5 | **Verbs — Irregular Verbs.** Base, simple past, and past participle. | Irregular verbs do not add *-ed*. After **has / have / had**, use the past participle. | Source table: begin–began–begun; speak–spoke–spoken; give–gave–given; see–saw–seen; go–went–gone; write–wrote–written; take–took–taken; eat–ate–eaten; ring–rang–rung; drink–drank–drunk. Traps: “had spoke” → “had spoken”; “gived” → “gave”; “had already began” → “had already begun.” | 3 |

**Grammar topic count:** 5 distinct lessons/topics, all explicitly represented in the source. The examples and special cases listed above are included in the lesson assignments.

## C. Reading Skills curriculum

The guide names seven reading skills on pages 4–6. They are distinct lessons because the question clues and answering strategies differ.

| Lesson | Skill and subskills | Strategy assigned to lesson | Source examples/passages | Source pages |
|---:|---|---|---|---:|
| 1 | **Central Idea and Theme:** central idea in informational text; theme in a story; whole-text scope. | Read title, first paragraph, and conclusion; notice repeated ideas; choose a full-text statement, not one detail. Avoid narrow, extreme (“always,” “fully proven”), or off-topic choices. State central idea/theme as a full sentence. | “Overcoming Impostor Syndrome” (informational) and story examples including “The Metamorphosis”; practice passages “The Courage to Be a Beginner,” “The Last Shift,” “Sleeping in Space,” and “The Myth of the Overnight Success.” | 4; practice 9–12 |
| 2 | **Evaluating an Argument:** claim, reasons, evidence, counterclaim, rebuttal. | Find what the speaker wants the audience to believe/do; locate the opposing view (e.g. “Some will say…”); examine how the speaker answers it, including acknowledging part before arguing back. | “We Choose to Go to the Moon” — John F. Kennedy. The guide notes the difficulty and expense objection and Kennedy’s answer that the difficulty itself is a reason to try. Practice passage: “The Courage to Be a Beginner,” paragraph 3. | 4; practice 9 |
| 3 | **Figurative Language and Word Choice:** metaphor, simile, symbol, tone, deeper meaning. | Identify the comparison; find what the compared things share; explain the meaning rather than the literal picture. For “MAINLY suggests,” look for deeper meaning. | “The Metamorphosis” — Gregor’s transformation can symbolize feeling alone and useless in his family. Practice imagery includes “quietly shrinks the world,” the sign “as if it were heavy,” “an invisible cloud,” and “messy beginnings” / “polished ending.” | 5; practice 9–12 |
| 4 | **Author’s Purpose and Point of View:** purpose (persuade, inform, explain); attitude/tone (certain, hopeful, critical, uncertain); function of details. | For “Why does the author include…?”, identify the detail’s job (example, contrast, support). Read certainty/tone words such as “may,” “remains an open question,” and “I believe.” Avoid unsupported extreme answers. | “We Choose to Go to the Moon” — John F. Kennedy; “may” language in “Sleeping in Space”; examples in “The Courage to Be a Beginner” and “The Myth of the Overnight Success.” | 5; practice 10–12 |
| 5 | **Text Structure:** paragraph purpose and relationship to surrounding paragraphs; signal words. | Summarize the paragraph in a few words; compare it with paragraphs before and after; name its role (evidence, balance, shift, etc.). Watch “however,” “yet,” “also,” “as a result,” and “in contrast.” | “A First in Space” (informational text elements); paragraph 3 of “Sleeping in Space” begins “Even with these solutions” and adds remaining problems. | 5; practice 11 |
| 6 | **Character and Setting:** character development through actions, words, thoughts, and others’ reactions; setting’s effect on choice/feeling. | Compare the character’s actions at the start and end; ask what caused change; consider how place or situation affects the character. | “The Metamorphosis” — observe the family’s treatment of Gregor and his room becoming the world he is trapped in. “The Last Shift” shows Mr. Idris’s decades-long routine and response to closing his shop. | 6; practice 10 |
| 7 | **Inference and Evidence:** implied conclusion; textual clues; supported reasoning; “MOST likely / MOST reasonably” stems. | Find clues in actions, words, and details; combine the clues into a logical conclusion; choose the best-supported answer and reject contradictions. | All Unit 1 texts. Practice inference includes Mr. Idris stopping Adel from turning the shop sign. | 6; practice 10 |

**Reading skill count:** 7 distinct skills, matching the source’s stated seven skills.
